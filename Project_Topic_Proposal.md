

##How do bees digest pollen?

Earth is home to over 20,000 bee species, which are major pollinators in both wild and agricultural ecosystems (Goulson et al. 2015). In the United States alone, bees provide more than $3 billion in crop pollination services annually (Kopec 2017). Unlike many other pollinators that visit flowers only for nectar, bees gather pollen as a source of nutrition for themselves and as the primary food for their brood (Kopec 2017). Pollen feeding("palynivory") is likely the key trait that spurred the evolution of bees from carnivorous wasp ancestors, enabling them to exploit a novel resource. Although palynivory is recognized as a defining feature of bee evolution, we know surprisingly little about one of the most fundamental aspects of bee biology: how bees digest pollen. The nutritional content of the bee diet is well understood: nectar provides simple carbohydrates, while pollen provides additional carbohydrates, protein, lipids, and essential micronutrients (Zheng et al. 2019). However, pollen nutrients are enclosed within a cell wall composed of sporopollenin, a highly recalcitrant organic material that presents a formidable digestive hurdle. Considering this challenge, how are bees digesting pollen? Discovering the mechanism will reveal the physiological adaptations underpinning the evolutionary origin of bees.  

##Plant cell wall degrading enzymes and proposed research

Research into bee palynivory has largely been explored in Western honeybees (_Apis mellifera_), a single species. Honey bees host a specialized core gut microbiome that  likely aids pollen digestion (Zheng et al. 2019), but is not representative for many taxa. Recent evidence from solitary bees, which account for 70% of bee diversity, report that bees often harbor sparse or highly variable gut microbiomes (Schlauch et al. 2016). The absence of a core gut microbiome raises the probability that a host-encoded mechanism is mediating pollen digestion in a majority of bees. One hypothesized mechanism underlying palynivory involves plant cell wall degrading enzymes (PCWDEs)(e.g., glycoside hydrolases, carbohydrate esterases, and polysaccharide lyases). In phytophagous beetles, PCWDEs enable efficient digestion of plant cell wall components such as cellulose, hemicellulose, and pectin (McKenna et al. 2019). In _A. mellifera_, endogenous PCWDEs produced by midgut epithelial cells are hypothesized to degrade and weaken the pollen cell wall. As a result, the nutrient rich cytoplasm is released through germination pores or the complete rupture of the pollen grain(Peng et al. 1986). Consequently, the cytoplasm is absorbed, completing the digestive process. Thus, palynivory may have been facilitated by the evolution of gut digestive enzymes that expanded bees' metabolic capabilities. Whether bees employ this mechanism remains untested. 

Comparative genomics provides an opportunity to investigate whether differences in feeding ecology between bees and wasps is associated with the evolution of digestive enzymes.  Here, I aim to **1: identify candidate plant cell wall degrading enzymes (PCWDEs)** and **2: compare their diversity and abundance between bees (n=6) and wasp (n=4) using genomic data**. I hypothesize that bee species will possess a greater diversity and abundance of PCWDEs than carnivorous wasps, reflecting their adaptation to pollen feeding. Including taxa from each of the six major bee families will also shed light on whether particular PCWDE families are shared or are potentially lineage specific. Following an existing bioinformatic protocol (Shin et al. 2026), I will use the dbCAN database to identify and annotate carbohydrate-active enzymes (CAZymes) in publicly available reference genomes sourced from the Beenome100 project, i5k initiative and NCBI GenBank. dbCAN is available as a web tool, but I will use the command line version in HPC3 to further develop my bash scripting skills. Putative CAZymes will be classified as cellulases, pectinases, or hemicellulases based on the functional data linked with each gene family in the CAZyme database. Following data curation, CAZymes will be imported into R for comparative analysis. I will use the R package pheatmap to construct a hierarchical clustered heatmap depicting CAZyme family enrichment across species. The heatmap will allow me to identify patterns of CAZyme family abundance  across species, and reveal whether bees share a similar repertoire of digestive enzymes that distinguishes them from wasps. I also will generate a functional scatterplot to map the total abundances of bee and wasp CAZyme families against each other (e.g., cellulases vs. pectinases). These comparisons may determine whether differences in digestive enzyme diversity are driven by particular functional categories.

##The Future

Beyond fulfilling the aims I have proposed, this project will establish a reproducible bioinformatic protocol for identifying candidate genes that may contribute to pollen digestion. This project would also produce preliminary data that will inform future research decisions. After identifying candidate PCWDEs, I will then use transcriptomics (RNA-Seq) to validate expression of PCWDEs in midgut tissue of laboratory-reared bumblebees (_Bombus impatiens__) in response to pollen consumption. To finally test the functional capabilities of the candidate PCWDEs, I will use RNA interference (RNAi) to suppress candidate genes and evaluate pollen digestion efficiency in _B.Impatiens__. This project, regardless of its outcome, will advance our understanding of gut function, a fundamental aspect of biology that is understudied in bees and wasps. In an evolutionary context, findings will also shed light on the molecular changes underlying the origin of herbivorous bees  from carnivorous wasp ancestors. Further, palynivory is widespread in a variety of other animal taxa beyond bees and wasps (Roulston & Cane 2000). Thus, this project will offer new insights into the potential mechanism by which other palynivores evolved to overcome the challenges of pollen feeding. In addition to my previous bioinformatic experience (e.g., de novo genome assembly, annotation, and figure building in R), I believe this project is feasible because of existing protocols in the cited literature and the easy access to public genomic data.
